Design Notes

The Approach

The three designs behind the Shoddy Apprenticeship — the method, the language, and the workshop — in one place.

Teaching a beginner to program in the AI era took three deliberate designs, and they only make sense together. The Treatment is the method: an apprenticeship that makes the learner earn understanding before they're allowed to automate it. The Language is the workbench: a purely functional BASIC small enough to hold in your head, built so that reading carefully is the same thing as knowing what happens. The Workshop is what the bench can do: real statistics, real optimization, a real database, graphics, and sound — capabilities worth building with, and therefore worth judging. Method, language, capability: who holds the pen, what the pen writes, and what's worth writing. First, though, the name — because in this project the metaphor is load-bearing.

Prologue · The Name

The Heritage

Where “shoddy” comes from, and why an apprenticeship wears a mill town's vocabulary.

In 1813, in Batley, a clothier named Benjamin Law worked out how to do something the wool trade had thought impossible: make new cloth from old. The towns around him — Batley, Dewsbury, Ossett, Morley, the “Heavy Woollen District” of the West Riding of Yorkshire — built an entire industry on the idea. Rags flowed in by the ton, were sorted by hand, by skill, by color and grade, and were fed to a spiked grinding machine the trade called the devil, which tore the old cloth back into loose fiber. That reclaimed fiber — shoddy — was blended with a proportion of new wool, spun, and woven into cloth again. It dressed the people virgin wool priced out: cheap suits, overcoats, blankets, and above all uniforms — half the world's armies fought in reclaimed Yorkshire wool. It was the American Civil War that ruined the word: Union contractors supplied recycled cloth so poor it reportedly dissolved in the rain, and “shoddy” crossed the Atlantic as a slur — unjust to the good mills of Batley and Dewsbury, which went on making honest cloth for another century, until synthetic fibers ended the trade. Nothing left the mill useless; even the unspinnable dust was sold to fertilize the hop gardens of Kent.

Why it ended After the Second World War the trade's foundations gave way from two directions at once. Nylon, polyester, and acrylic made new fiber cheaper than reclaimed wool — erasing shoddy's whole reason to exist — and, more fatally, synthetics poisoned the feedstock itself: once the rag stream filled with blended fabrics that could be neither sorted into pure wool nor torn back into spinnable fiber, the devil had nothing left it could digest. Tastes moved to lighter cloth in centrally heated homes; armies went synthetic. By the 1980s an industry that had run for a century and a half was gone — just as the wider world began rediscovering, under the name “textile recycling,” the thing the West Riding had industrialized in 1813.

The analogy runs deeper than the name. Shoddy-the-language was made the way shoddy-the-cloth was: almost nothing in it is new fiber. BASIC's keywords, Forth's stack, Joy's quotations, QuickBASIC's Type, ISAM from the mainframe age — old cloth, gathered and sorted. The lexer is the devil, tearing source into fibers; desugaring is the carding and re-spinning that turns surface syntax into core thread; a proportion of new wool — closures, pattern matching, immutable Let — is blended in, because pure reclamation was never the whole recipe. The mill weaves it, the machines are its equipment, programs are the cloth, and the golden test files are the cloth-hall inspection that decides whether a piece is true. Even the slur is part of the inheritance: the name invites the world to expect rubbish, and the product's job is to be better than its reputation — the oldest trick in Batley.

The apprenticeship simply completes the picture. A West Riding mill was staffed the way all skilled trades were: by apprentices at a bench beside a craftsman, learning by hand what could not be learned by watching — sorting rags is a skill, and so is judging cloth. That is the whole curriculum in one image: the apprentice at the workbench, the Craftsman beside them, and the standing test of the trade — can you look at a finished piece and tell whether it's honest? The vocabulary on every page of this document — mill, machines, devil, apprentice, Craftsman, cloth — isn't decoration. It is a reminder that the project's motto describes both the cloth and the learner: making useless things useful, through skill.

The industry's ending is the analogy's quiet warning, already heeded: when the old substrate could no longer sustain the process, the answer was not to stop reclaiming but to move to new fiber — which is what the .NET mill is.

Part I · Curriculum Design

The Treatment

How a beginner learns to program in the AI era: by earning the right to delegate.

The problem

A beginner learning to code today faces a strange trap. The AI will write anything they ask for — so they never struggle, never trace code in their head, and never build the one skill that now matters most: looking at code someone else wrote and knowing whether to trust it. They can generate endlessly and judge nothing. That's not a developer; that's a passenger.

The idea

The Shoddy Apprenticeship flips the modern learning loop back into the right order. The learner is the apprentice. The teacher is the Craftsman — an AI given strict standing instructions to behave like a seasoned tradesperson at a bench, never like a chatbot that hands out answers. The workbench is Shoddy, a small, purely functional BASIC built for exactly this job: readable line-one syntax, no framework noise, no Stack Overflow escape hatch, and a difficulty curve we fully control because we own the language.

One principle runs through everything: understanding must be earned before it is automated. The apprentice writes every line by hand first, so that by the time they're allowed to prompt the Craftsman for code, they can judge what comes back. Validation is the skill; generation is the reward.

The whole arc in one sentence: write it yourself → read what others wrote → guide the Craftsman → validate the Craftsman → own the outcome.

How it works

Five phases, each shifting who holds the pen:

  1. Manual Craft — the apprentice writes 100% of the code. The Craftsman demonstrates on different problems and coaches with questions, never answers. (Seven lessons, fully written.)
  2. Reading the Craft — the apprentice critiques code they didn't write, including deliberately flawed code. Training the eye.
  3. Guided Generation — the apprentice writes the spec, the Craftsman drafts half-finished code, the apprentice completes and verifies it.
  4. Prompt & Validate — the modern working loop: the apprentice prompts for whole functions, then validates rigorously with tests the Craftsman didn't write.
  5. Ownership — multi-part projects where the apprentice directs, reviews, and takes full accountability for code they didn't type.

Every lesson follows the same eight-step ritual: demonstration, challenge, predict-before-you-run (a hard gate — no running code blind, ever), productive struggle, run & validate, critique, reflection, and one durable takeaway. The Craftsman operates under a contract it never breaks: no unexplained solutions, no code-writing in Phases 1–2, never “wrong” — always “not yet.”

The design isn't invented from scratch. It's the Cognitive Apprenticeship model — modeling, coaching, scaffolding that fades as skill grows — plus two well-established levers: productive struggle (effort makes learning stick) and worked-example fading. What's new is aiming all of it at the skill the AI era actually demands.

The investment

The real price The apprentice must accept struggle as the method. They will write a prediction before every single run. They will sit with an error message instead of being rescued. They will explain their reasoning out loud. The Craftsman is engineered to withhold the answer when the apprentice is one step from finding it — because that step is where the learning is stored. An apprentice (or parent) shopping for frictionless progress should look elsewhere; this is deliberately the opposite.

The expected results

By the end of Phase 1, the apprentice can write, run, and debug real programs by hand: decisions, repetition without loops, their own functions, recursion, and Map/Filter/Fold — and they've internalized the course's two standing habits: predict before you run and the bugs live on the boundaries.

By the end of Phase 5, the apprentice works the way a modern developer actually works: they can break a problem down, write an unambiguous spec, direct an AI to draft code, read the result critically, write their own tests to catch the plausible-but-wrong, and take ownership of the final product. The durable outcome isn't Shoddy fluency — it's transferable judgment: the difference between someone who can ask an AI for code and someone who can be responsible for it.

That judgment is the whole point. Generation is cheap now. Trust is not.

Part II · Language Design

The Language

Shoddy, briefly — what it means to be purely functional, and why the workbench is built that way.

What it is

Shoddy is a purely functional BASIC. On the surface it reads like the friendliest language ever put in front of a beginner: BASIC's keywords, Python's indentation, parenthesized calls, infix arithmetic. Underneath, every program compiles onto a Forth/Joy-style stack core — and then all the way down to real .NET, woven to C# and compiled with Roslyn. It is a small language you can hold entirely in your head, that nonetheless runs as a genuine compiled program.

Type Shape = Circle(r) | Rect(w, h)

Def Area(s As Shape) As Number
    Select Case s
        Case Circle(r)
            Pi() * r ^ 2
        Case Rect(w, h)
            w * h

Everything in this part follows from one decision: nothing ever changes.

Purely functional — what that means here

There is no assignment. Let binds a name to a value once, forever — rebinding it is an error, not a quiet overwrite. There is no for, no while, no goto. “Updating” a record or an array hands back a new value; the old one is untouched. Repetition is done by describing it — Each, Times, recursion, and the big three: Map, Filter, Fold. The few real effects — Print, Input, Rnd, file words — are kept at the edges of the program by convention.

Why: when nothing mutates, a value can never change behind your back. What a name held on the line where you bound it is what it holds on every line after. That single guarantee is what makes code traceable in your head — you can predict a program's output by reading it, which is the entire learning method. It also removes the classic beginner-killer: the tangle of shifting loop variables. And it isn't a performance trap: self-recursive tail calls compile to loops, so recursion runs in constant stack space.

A deliberately small set of types

TypeNotes
NumberThe only numeric type (an IEEE double; exact for whole numbers to 253). No int/long/float/decimal zoo.
StringImmutable text.
BooleanTrue/False — and never interchangeable with numbers. No truthiness.
QuotationA function as a value — a lexical closure. Lambdas (Fn(x) =>) and bare function names passed as arguments are these.
List / ArrayTwo sequences, honestly priced: lists are cheap to walk and grow at the front (built for recursion); arrays are O(1) to index. Neither mutates.
Records via TypeYour own data shapes — see below.

And no NULL. There is no nothing-value to sneak into a variable and detonate three functions later. Absence is modeled explicitly — an empty list, or a sum type like Some(v) | None — where the language can make you handle it.

Why so few: every type a language adds is a conversion rule, a coercion trap, and an equality surprise the learner must memorize. Shoddy spends that budget on concepts that generalize — functions, sequences, records — not on numeric width. A beginner can know the whole type system by the end of week one; an expert never hits “works with int, breaks with long.”

No objects, no classes

A Type declaration makes plain, transparent data — not an object:

Type Person
    Name As String
    Age  As Number

That one declaration gives you a constructor (Person("ANN", 34)), an accessor per field (Name(p), Age(p)), and functional update (With(p, Age = 35) — a new person, one field different). There are no methods, no inheritance, no this, no hidden state inside the value. Accessors are ordinary functions, so they compose like everything else: Map(people, Name).

Why: objects bundle data with behavior and then hide both behind a wall of methods — which is precisely the wrong first mental model. In Shoddy, data is data and functions are functions; what a value is and what you can do with it are always separately visible. The entire apparatus of OO — constructors vs. initializers, overriding, equals-and-hashCode contracts — simply has nothing to attach to.

One equality

In most languages a beginner meets two equalities almost immediately — “same object in memory” vs. “same contents” (== vs. .equals(), is vs. ==) — and the distinction only matters because objects can mutate: two references to one changing thing are different from two things that merely look alike today.

Shoddy has one =, and it is structural, recursive, and always right: Person("ANN", 34) = Person("ANN", 34) is True, however each was built. Since nothing can ever change, “looks the same” and “is the same” are permanently the same question — identity-vs-value equality isn't solved, it's dissolved. Records even print as valid input syntax (Person(Name = "ANN", Age = 34)): what you see is the entire truth about the value.

Pattern matching & discriminated unions

A sum type says a value is one of a fixed set of shapes: Type Shape = Circle(r) | Rect(w, h). And Select Case — QBasic's old friend, grown up — is how you take them apart. It matches plain values, ranges (Case 4 To 10), comparisons (Case Is >= 90), and destructures records — naming the pieces, nesting to any depth, with Where guards for the fine print:

Select Case p
    Case Person(n, a) Where a < 18
        n & " IS A MINOR"
    Case Person(n, _)
        n & " IS AN ADULT"

Why this pair matters: together they turn “handle every possibility” from a discipline into a habit. The type declares exactly which cases exist; the match walks them; and if a value slips through with no matching Case and no Case Else, the program stops and says so — loudly, with a line number — instead of limping on. That is the “enumerate the cases, hunt the boundaries” thinking the curriculum drills, built into the language itself. It's also how you live well without NULL: Some(v) | None plus a match is the missing-value story.

The honest machine underneath

All of the friendly surface desugars by a handful of mechanical rules onto a concatenative stack core — f(a, b) is a b f, an If is two quotations and a choice — and that core is itself a legal dialect you can write directly (Def Square ( Number -- Number ) / Dup *). Run mill gen and read the actual C# your program became.

Why keep it exposed: no magic. When the apprentice eventually asks “but what does the machine actually do with this?”, the answer isn't hand-waving — it's one mill gen away, and the desugaring rules stay honest because the target is a language you can actually write.

The through-line: every one of these choices removes a way for a program to surprise its reader. What's left is a language where reading carefully is the same thing as knowing what happens — which is exactly the skill the apprenticeship exists to teach.

Part III · Technology Design

The Workshop

What the workbench can actually do — math, statistics, optimization, storage, graphics, sound — and why an apprentice needs real capabilities, not toy ones.

The claim

Learning languages are traditionally allowed to be useless. The vocabulary is small, the programs are exercises, and the moment a learner wants to make something — a chart, a game, a tune, a file that remembers — they're told to graduate to a real language, where the capability lives inside frameworks, package managers, and half a million lines of someone else's code. That handoff is exactly where most beginners are lost.

Shoddy refuses the trade. The language is deliberately small — an apprentice holds all of it in their head by week one (Part II). The workshop is deliberately not: twenty machines and a set of built-in devices cover arithmetic through inferential statistics, linear programming, a keyed on-disk database, a graphics window, turtles, charts, terminals, and a synthesizer that plays sheet music. Real capabilities matter to the apprenticeship for three reasons:

  • Motivation is fuel. A program that draws, sings, saves, or decides something is a program worth finishing — and worth debugging. Nobody sits with an error message for twenty minutes to fix an exercise; they will for their own game.
  • Judgment needs something to judge. The whole arc (Part I) trains the apprentice to validate code — theirs, then the Craftsman's. Validation is only a real skill when programs have real, observable outcomes: a picture that's subtly wrong, a p-value that's suspiciously clean, a database that loses a record. Toy domains train toy judgment.
  • The library is the reading curriculum. Every machine is written in Shoddy, in the same few pages of vocabulary the apprentice already has. The chart they just drew, the database they just queried — they can open it and read how. Phase 2 (“reading the craft”) doesn't need a synthetic corpus; the standard library is one.

The design rule, in one line: small language, big workshop — every concept earns its keep, and every capability is one Include away, with readable source and no framework noise between the apprentice and the machine.

Numbers, chance, and money

The numeric floor is honest and flat: one Number type, the standard function set, and a math machine that adds the words games and graphics are actually written in — Clamp, Lerp, Remap, Smoothstep, Dist. Chance is a first-class citizen (Shuffle, Sample, Chance), and it is seedable — which quietly teaches a professional habit: a random program you cannot replay is a program you cannot test.

Why it matters for an apprentice: the money machine is the sharpest lesson in the cluster — currency as whole cents, because floating-point drift is real and representation is a choice with consequences. A learner who has watched 0.1 + 0.2 misbehave and then fixed it by changing the representation has learned something no toy curriculum teaches: the machine's number is not the mathematician's number, and engineering is managing that gap.

Statistics — the science of “is it real?”

The stats machine goes far past averages: quantiles, correlation, regression, and the classic inference tests — t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square, confidence intervals — producing real p-values from the special-function builtins, not lookup-table approximations.

Why it matters for an apprentice: this is the curriculum's own core skill wearing a lab coat. The apprenticeship trains one question above all — should I trust this? — and statistics is that question made rigorous: is the difference real or is it noise? An apprentice who has run a t-test on their own experiment's numbers carries the habit back into Phase 4, where they must decide whether the Craftsman's code merely looks right. Data literacy and code skepticism are the same muscle.

Optimization — wishing under constraints

The simplex machine solves linear programs — minimize a cost subject to constraints — and reports not just the answer but the economics of it: shadow prices per constraint, reduced costs per variable. The mps machine reads the industry's interchange format, so problems from the real optimization world load directly.

Why it matters for an apprentice: optimization is modeling practice. To use it at all, the apprentice must translate a fuzzy wish (“the cheapest diet that keeps me fed”) into precise costs and constraints — and that act of translation is spec-writing, the exact skill Phases 3–5 demand when directing the Craftsman. An unambiguous problem statement is the price of a trustworthy answer, from a solver or from an AI. The solver just grades you faster.

Memory — files up to a real database

Storage climbs an honest ladder: whole text files (builtins), line-oriented files (file), fixed-length binary records with computable offsets (recio), and finally isam — a genuine keyed database with its index kept on disk as a B+tree, insert/update/delete/range-scan included. Each rung is built visibly on the one below, and all of it is readable Shoddy.

Why it matters for an apprentice: persistence is what separates a program from a demo — a grade book that forgets its students on exit isn't a tool. And the ladder demystifies the most mythologized object in computing: a database stops being an acronym vendor and becomes a file, an index, and arithmetic the apprentice has personally read. Nothing fights the “it's magic, don't look inside” instinct better than looking inside.

Graphics — errors you can see

The scribbler is a window of pixels with one primitive — colour this dot — and everything else (Bresenham lines, midpoint circles, flood fill) built on top in plain Shoddy. The turtle walks Logo's pen over it; the plotter turns lists of numbers into histograms, bar charts, pies, box plots, and scatter plots.

Why it matters for an apprentice: graphics gives the feedback loop teeth. Predict before you run — the course's hard gate — becomes literal and unfakeable when the prediction is a shape: a polygon that doesn't close or a circle that's lopsided is a bug the apprentice can see, point at, and reason backward from. The turtle carries fifty years of evidence (Logo put it in classrooms for exactly this reason) that walking a pen around a screen makes angles, distances, and decomposition tangible. And the plotter closes a loop with the statistics chapter: numbers the apprentice computed become pictures the apprentice can distrust at a glance.

Sound — the mill's voice

Play(1, "T120 O3 L4 G G G L8 D# E L4 A# G L8 D# E L2 A#")
Sound(NoteFreq("A5"), 60)             ' a game blip, by note name
NoteOn(2, MidiFreq(57 + x / 20))    ' a held note gliding under the mouse

The buzzer is the mill's sound device: fire-and-forget blips, held notes that bend under a mouse, and queued tunes written in the same Music Macro Language GW-BASIC taught a generation to type in from magazines. Underneath, eight channels and a voice pool — the sound-chip model, mixed in software.

Play is only the top of the kit. The machine also speaks music's own vocabularies: note names (NoteFreq("C4") — middle C, scientific pitch) and MIDI numbers (MidiFreq is equal temperament in one line, and it takes fractional numbers, so quarter-tones and pitch bends are just arithmetic). The builtins underneath are their own instruments: Sound for game effects, NoteOn/NoteOff for a synthesizer — retuning a sounding channel glides with no re-attack, which is how the demo turns a mouse drag into a glissando — SoundQueue for sample-accurate melody, SoundGain for balance. And the score itself is honest data: MmlNotes is a pure parser, MML string in, list of notes out — a tune can be Mapped (transpose it), reversed, tested headless with no speaker at all, then handed to PlayNotes.

Why it matters for an apprentice: partly delight — a program that plays the opening of Beethoven's Fifth is a program someone shows their family, and showing is finishing. But the API also teaches two quietly deep ideas. The three sound words are three answers to who owns this note's lifetime — the runtime (a blip), the program (a held note), or the score (a queue); ownership-of-a-resource is one of programming's permanent questions, and here it's audible. And because a score is just a list of values, music becomes one more thing the functional toolkit already handles — the Map that pulled names from people in Part II transposes a melody here.

Interaction — programs that respond

Scribbler programs receive events — mouse, keys, typed characters, timer ticks — as one pattern-matchable value, and the standard shape of a game is a Select Case inside a function that calls itself with the next state. The keys machine classifies raw key codes; InKey and vt100 do the same for the terminal, with the real DEC escape codes of 1978.

Why it matters for an apprentice: the event loop is where the purely functional model proves itself on a real workload. State that seems to demand mutation — the paddle's position, the held note, the score — is just an argument passed to the next call of the loop. An apprentice who has built a game this way has felt, not been told, that “no variables” scales past exercises. It is also where capstones live: every Phase 5 project worth owning — a game, a synth, a visualizer — is an event loop with the rest of the workshop plugged into it.

Why real capabilities, and why now

Every domain above is the genuine article scaled to a bench, not a cartoon of one: real p-values, a real simplex tableau, a real B+tree, real escape codes, real equal temperament. That choice compounds through the whole apprenticeship:

  • Transfer. What the apprentice learns is the domain, not a curriculum's imitation of it. A t-test, a linear program, an index — these mean the same thing everywhere for the rest of their career.
  • No escape hatch, no noise. One Include per capability; no package manager, no versions, no framework ceremony. Nothing stands between the apprentice and the machine — and nothing on Stack Overflow will do the lesson for them.
  • Judgment scales with the programs. By Phase 4 the apprentice is validating Craftsman-written code against outcomes that can actually be wrong in interesting ways — a chart, a ledger, a solver's answer — which is precisely the working loop of a modern developer.

A toy language teaches you to pass its course. A small language with a real workshop teaches you to build — and in the AI era, to check the work.
