# The Heritage of the Name

*Making useless things useful, through skill.*

## The mills

In 1813, in Batley, a clothier named Benjamin Law worked out how to do
something the wool trade had thought impossible: make new cloth from old.
The towns around him — Batley, Dewsbury, Ossett, Morley, the "Heavy
Woollen District" of the West Riding of Yorkshire — built an entire
industry on the idea. Rags flowed in from everywhere: from rag-and-bone
men working British streets, and by the ton from Europe and beyond,
sorted (largely by women, by hand, by color and grade — a skill in
itself) and then fed to a spiked, snarling grinding machine the trade
called the **devil**, which tore the old cloth back into loose fiber.
Soft rags — old knitwear, stockings — yielded *shoddy*; hard, felted
tailor's cloth, conquered a couple of decades later by a fiercer
process, yielded the finer *mungo*, a Batley coinage. The reclaimed
fiber was blended with a proportion of new wool, carded, spun on mules,
and woven into cloth again.

## The products

Shoddy cloth dressed the people that virgin wool priced out. It became
cheap suits, overcoats, and blankets for the working class at home and
for export; pilot cloth and druggets; and above all, uniforms — the
Crimean War, the Franco-Prussian War, and both World Wars were fought
substantially in reclaimed Yorkshire wool, and the mills' order books
rose and fell with the world's armies. It was the American Civil War
that ruined the word: Union contractors supplied uniforms of such poor
recycled cloth that they reportedly dissolved in the rain, and "shoddy"
crossed the Atlantic as a slur — which was always a little unjust to
Batley and Dewsbury, where the good mills made honest cloth and their
owners' fortunes built town halls and chapels. Even the waste had a
use: unspinnable dust was sold as fertilizer for the hop gardens of
Kent. Nothing left the mill useless.

## The end

After the Second World War the trade's foundations gave way from two
directions at once. Nylon, polyester, and acrylic made *new* fiber
cheaper than reclaimed wool, erasing shoddy's whole reason to exist —
and, more fatally, synthetics poisoned the feedstock itself: once the
rag stream filled with blended fabrics that could not be sorted into
pure wool nor torn back into spinnable fiber, the devil had nothing
left it could digest. Tastes moved to lighter cloth in centrally heated
homes; armies went synthetic. The mills thinned through the 1950s and
60s, and by the 1980s an industry that had run for a century and a half
was effectively gone — just as the wider world began rediscovering,
under the name "textile recycling," the thing the West Riding had
industrialized in 1813.

## The language

The analogy runs deeper than the name. Shoddy-the-language was made the
way shoddy-the-cloth was: nothing in it is new fiber. BASIC's keywords,
Forth's stack, Joy's quotations, QuickBASIC's `Type`, VB's `IsEmpty`,
ISAM from the mainframe age — old cloth, gathered and sorted. The lexer
is the devil, tearing source into fibers; desugaring is the carding and
re-spinning that turns surface syntax into core thread; a proportion of
new wool — closures, pattern matching, root `Let` — is blended in,
because pure reclamation was never the whole recipe. The **mill** weaves
it, the **machines** are its equipment, programs are the cloth, and the
golden files are the cloth-hall inspection that decides whether a piece
is true. Even the slur is part of the inheritance: the name invites the
world to expect rubbish, and the product's job is to be better than its
reputation — the oldest trick in Batley. And the industry's ending is
the analogy's quiet warning, already heeded: when the old substrate
could no longer sustain the process, the answer was not to stop
reclaiming but to move to new fiber — which is what the .NET mill is.
